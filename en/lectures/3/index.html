Stress and energy flux

What this lecture does

We derive the scalar stress tensor from translations and prove the energy balance on rectangular regions. We compute the energy carried out by a massless pulse and compare the stress tensor with metric variation.

An oscillator conserves its energy when its Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence. A field can also carry energy from one place to another. Time translation gives a local balance law. The energy in a fixed rectangle is constant on a time interval exactly when the net outward flux through its faces vanishes throughout that interval.

3.1 Translation currents

We use the coordinates and metric of Lecture 1 on M=R×RdM=\R\times\R^dM=R×Rd or M=R×TdM=\R\times\torus^dM=R×Td. A density is autonomous if it has no explicit dependence on xxx. The momentum current remains pμ=∂L/∂(∂μϕ)\momcurrent{\mu}=\partial\Lag/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)pμ=∂L/∂(∂μ​ϕ).

Theorem 3.3 (Scalar stress and energy balance). For V∈C2(R)V\in C^2(\R)V∈C2(R) and ϕ∈C2(M,R)\phi\in C^2(M,\R)ϕ∈C2(M,R) with density (3.2), translations give (3.1), with ∂μTμν=−E[ϕ]∂νϕ=(□ϕ+V′(ϕ))∂νϕ.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:offstress} \partial_\mu\stress^\mu{}_\nu =-\EL[\phi]\partial_\nu\phi =\bigl(\dAlembert\phi+V'(\phi)\bigr)\partial_\nu\phi . \end{equation}∂μ​Tμν​=−E[ϕ]∂ν​ϕ=(□ϕ+V′(ϕ))∂ν​ϕ.(3.6) If ϕ\phiϕ solves □ϕ+V′(ϕ)=0\dAlembert\phi+V'(\phi)=0□ϕ+V′(ϕ)=0, then ∂tρen+∂ifi=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:continuity} \partial_t\rho_{\mathrm{en}}+\partial_i f^i=0 . \end{equation}∂t​ρen​+∂i​fi=0.(3.7) On a fixed rectangle Ω⊂Rd\Omega\subset\R^dΩ⊂Rd, its energy satisfies ddtEen,Ω(t)=−FΩ(t),Een,Ω(t1)−Een,Ω(t0)=−∫t0t1FΩ(t) dt.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:balance} \frac{\dd}{\dd t}E_{\mathrm{en},\Omega}(t)=-\mathcal F_\Omega(t), \qquad E_{\mathrm{en},\Omega}(t_1)-E_{\mathrm{en},\Omega}(t_0) =-\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathcal F_\Omega(t)\,\dd t . \end{equation}dtd​Een,Ω​(t)=−FΩ​(t),Een,Ω​(t1​)−Een,Ω​(t0​)=−∫t0​t1​​FΩ​(t)dt.(3.8) On Td\torus^dTd, the total energy is constant.

Proof. The translation argument above identifies the current. We verify its divergence directly and then integrate one coordinate at a time.

First, differentiating the scalar density along xνx^\nuxν gives ∂νL=∂μϕ ∂ν∂μϕ−V′(ϕ)∂νϕ.\partial_\nu\Lag =\partial^\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\phi-V'(\phi)\partial_\nu\phi .∂ν​L=∂μϕ∂ν​∂μ​ϕ−V′(ϕ)∂ν​ϕ. On a solution its right-hand side is zero. Taking ν=0\nu=0ν=0 gives (3.7), since Ti0=Ti0=fi\stress^i{}_0=\stress^{i0}=f^iTi0​=Ti0=fi.

Next, ρen\rho_{\mathrm{en}}ρen​ and fif^ifi are C1C^1C1. On each compact time interval, the parameter-integral theorem recalled in Lecture 1 permits ddt∫Ωρen=∫Ω∂tρen\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int_\Omega\rho_{\mathrm{en}}=\int_\Omega\partial_t\rho_{\mathrm{en}}dtd​∫Ω​ρen​=∫Ω​∂t​ρen​. Finally, on Td\torus^dTd integrate over [0,2π]d[0,2\pi]^d[0,2π]d. Opposite face values agree by periodicity, so every difference in (3.5) vanishes. 

For a solution on Rd\R^dRd whose first derivatives ϕt\phi_tϕt​ and ∇ϕ\nabla\phi∇ϕ vanish outside one fixed compact spatial set throughout [t0,t1][t_0,t_1][t0​,t1​], and with V(ϕ)=0V(\phi)=0V(ϕ)=0 outside that set, a containing rectangle has zero boundary flux. Its energy is the total energy, so the total energy is constant on that interval. For more general decay, the cutoff proof in Lecture 2 applies to the energy current under the density and spatial-flux integrability hypotheses stated there.

3.4 Variation of a background metric

The scalar tensor also describes how the action changes when the metric changes. This description will be used for the gravitational field in Lecture 28. Here the metric is prescribed, and the scalar field stays fixed during the variation.

Let gμν(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x)gμν​(x) be a C1C^1C1 symmetric nondegenerate matrix of signature (+,−,…,−)(+,-,\dots,-)(+,−,…,−), with inverse gμνg^{\mu\nu}gμν. Write g=det⁡(gμν)g=\det(g_{\mu\nu})g=det(gμν​) and volg=∣g∣ vol\vol_g=\sqrt{|g|}\,\volvolg​=∣g∣​vol. On a compact coordinate region DDD the scalar action is SD[ϕ,g]=∫DLg volg,Lg=12gμν∂μϕ ∂νϕ−V(ϕ).\action_D[\phi,g]=\int_D\Lag_g\,\vol_g, \qquad \Lag_g=\frac12 g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi-V(\phi) .SD​[ϕ,g]=∫D​Lg​volg​,Lg​=21​gμν∂μ​ϕ∂ν​ϕ−V(ϕ). We vary the inverse metric by a symmetric C1C^1C1 array kμνk^{\mu\nu}kμν with compact support in the interior of DDD. For sufficiently small ε\varepsilonε, gμν+εkμνg^{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon k^{\mu\nu}gμν+εkμν remains nondegenerate with the same signature.

Definition 3.4 (Hilbert stress tensor). The Hilbert stress tensor is the continuous symmetric array TμνH\stress^{\mathrm H}_{\mu\nu}TμνH​ defined by the response to inverse-metric variation: δgSD[ϕ,g]k=12∫DTμνHkμν volg.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:hilbertdef} \delta_g\action_D[\phi,g]k =\frac12\int_D\stress^{\mathrm H}_{\mu\nu}k^{\mu\nu}\,\vol_g . \end{equation}δg​SD​[ϕ,g]k=21​∫D​TμνH​kμνvolg​.(3.11)

The variations range over symmetric arrays. The factor 12\tfrac1221​ is part of the convention, including for off-diagonal entries. If two continuous symmetric arrays give the same response for every such variation, apply the fundamental lemma of Lecture 1 to each diagonal entry and to each symmetric pair of entries. Their difference is zero, so this definition determines at most one tensor.
