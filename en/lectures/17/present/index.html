Lecture 17

Vacua and Goldstone modes

We prove the orbit–Hessian Goldstone lemma for a compact group acting orthogonally on real scalar fields. We compute the radial and phase masses of the global O(2)O(2)O(2) model with the course normalisation.

The curvature of a potential at equilibrium gives an oscillator’s squared frequency. For a field, the same curvature gives squared masses. A continuous symmetry can force some of these curvatures to vanish. We identify the directions it forces and distinguish them from other zero curvatures.

17.1 Vacua and the linearised field

Let V:RN→RV:\R^N\to\RV:RN→R be C2C^2C2 and attain its minimum value Vmin⁡V_{\min}Vmin​.

Definition 17.1 (Vacuum and vacuum set). A vacuum is a constant field ϕ(x)=u0\phi(x)=u_0ϕ(x)=u0​ with V(u0)=Vmin⁡V(u_0)=V_{\min}V(u0​)=Vmin​. The vacuum set is V={u∈RN:V(u)=Vmin⁡}.\mathcal V=\{u\in\R^N:V(u)=V_{\min}\}.V={u∈RN:V(u)=Vmin​}. When this set is a smooth submanifold, it is called the vacuum manifold.

The set of minima need not be a manifold: V(u1,u2)=u12u22V(u_1,u_2)=u_1^2u_2^2V(u1​,u2​)=u12​u22​ has two crossing axes as its minima. Each compact group orbit considered below is a smooth submanifold of RN\R^NRN contained in the vacuum set. As in Lecture 15, a nonzero constant background on Rd\R^dRd need not belong to L2L^2L2. The perturbation, rather than the background, supplies the linear evolution data.

Use the canonical real-scalar density and equation of Lecture 1: L=12∑a=1N∂μϕa∂μϕa−V(ϕ),□ϕ+∇V(ϕ)=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:field} \Lag=\frac12\sum_{a=1}^N\partial_\mu\phi^a\partial^\mu\phi^a-V(\phi), \qquad \dAlembert\phi+\nabla V(\phi)=0. \end{equation}L=21​a=1∑N​∂μ​ϕa∂μϕa−V(ϕ),□ϕ+∇V(ϕ)=0.(17.1) Fix a vacuum u0∈Vu_0\in\mathcal Vu0​∈V. Its gradient vanishes. Differentiating this equation at ϕ=u0\phi=u_0ϕ=u0​ in the direction hhh gives the linearised equation □h+Mh=0,M=D2V(u0).\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:linear} \dAlembert h+\mathsf M h=0, \qquad \mathsf M=D^2V(u_0). \end{equation}□h+Mh=0,M=D2V(u0​).(17.2)

Definition 17.2 (Mass matrix and massless mode). The Hessian M\mathsf MM in (17.2) is the mass matrix, whose eigenvalues are the squared masses. An eigenvector with eigenvalue zero is a massless mode of the linearised field. Modes are counted with their linear multiplicities.

The matrix is real symmetric. For every w∈RNw\in\R^Nw∈RN, the second derivative of s↦V(u0+sw)s\mapsto V(u_0+sw)s↦V(u0​+sw) at zero is wTMw≥0w^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M w\ge0wTMw≥0. Thus its eigenvalues are ma2≥0m_a^2\ge0ma2​≥0. An orthogonal change of field components diagonalises the equation into (□+ma2)ha=0(\dAlembert+m_a^2)h_a=0(□+ma2​)ha​=0. The Fourier frequencies are νa(k)=∣k∣2+ma2\nu_a(k)=\sqrt{|k|^2+m_a^2}νa​(k)=∣k∣2+ma2​​. These equations have the energy-class evolution of Lecture 4. The number of massless components is therefore dim⁡ker⁡M\dim\ker\mathsf MdimkerM.

17.2 The symmetry orbit and its Hessian

Let GGG be a compact finite-dimensional Lie group and let R:G→O(N)\mathcal R:G\to O(N)R:G→O(N) be a smooth group homomorphism. It defines the linear action u↦R(g)uu\mapsto\mathcal R(g)uu↦R(g)u. Assume that V(R(g)u)=V(u)(g∈G, u∈RN).\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:invariance} V(\mathcal R(g)u)=V(u)\qquad(g\in G,\ u\in\R^N). \end{equation}V(R(g)u)=V(u)(g∈G, u∈RN).(17.3) Orthogonality also preserves the kinetic term of (17.1), so this is a global internal symmetry of the field theory.

Definition 17.3 (Unbroken subgroup and orbit directions). The unbroken subgroup at u0u_0u0​ is its stabiliser H={g∈G:R(g)u0=u0}.H=\{g\in G:\mathcal R(g)u_0=u_0\}.H={g∈G:R(g)u0​=u0​}. The vacuum orbit is Ou0={R(g)u0:g∈G}\mathcal O_{u_0}=\{\mathcal R(g)u_0:g\in G\}Ou0​​={R(g)u0​:g∈G}. For XXX in the Lie algebra g=TeG\mathfrak g=T_eGg=Te​G, let TX=dRe(X)T_X=d\mathcal R_e(X)TX​=dRe​(X). Its orbit direction is TXu0T_Xu_0TX​u0​. The continuous direction is unbroken when TXu0=0T_Xu_0=0TX​u0​=0 and broken otherwise.

Differentiating R(g)TR(g)=I\mathcal R(g)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal R(g)=IR(g)TR(g)=I at the identity gives TXT=−TXT_X^{\mathsf T}=-T_XTXT​=−TX​. The stabiliser is closed, since it is the inverse image of u0u_0u0​ under the continuous orbit map. Recall the closed-subgroup quotient theorem from Topology and Geometry, Part I, Lecture 8: a closed subgroup is a Lie subgroup, and G/HG/HG/H is a smooth manifold of dimension dim⁡G−dim⁡H\dim G-\dim HdimG−dimH. The action need not be faithful; elements acting trivially belong to HHH. We also recall from Part I, Lecture 9, that exp⁡(sX)\exp(sX)exp(sX) is the one-parameter subgroup with initial tangent XXX. Differentiating its representation gives the matrix equation Y′=TXYY'=T_XYY′=TX​Y, hence R(exp⁡(sX))=exp⁡(sTX)\mathcal R(\exp(sX))=\exp(sT_X)R(exp(sX))=exp(sTX​) by uniqueness of that linear ODE.

Lemma 17.4 (Orbit–Hessian Goldstone lemma). Under these hypotheses, the orbit is a smooth embedded submanifold Ou0≅G/H\mathcal O_{u_0}\cong G/HOu0​​≅G/H contained in V\mathcal VV, with Tu0Ou0={TXu0:X∈g}⊂ker⁡D2V(u0),dim⁡Tu0Ou0=dim⁡G−dim⁡H.\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:kernel} \begin{gathered} T_{u_0}\mathcal O_{u_0}=\{T_Xu_0:X\in\mathfrak g\} \subset\ker D^2V(u_0),\\ \dim T_{u_0}\mathcal O_{u_0}=\dim G-\dim H. \end{gathered} \end{equation}Tu0​​Ou0​​={TX​u0​:X∈g}⊂kerD2V(u0​),dimTu0​​Ou0​​=dimG−dimH.​(17.4) Consequently the linearised field has at least dim⁡G−dim⁡H\dim G-\dim HdimG−dimH massless modes. It has exactly that many if and only if the Hessian kernel equals the orbit tangent space.

Proof. We first identify the tangent space and its dimension. The map G/H→RNG/H\to\R^NG/H→RN given by gH↦R(g)u0gH\mapsto\mathcal R(g)u_0gH↦R(g)u0​ is well-defined and injective: two group elements have the same image exactly when they differ by an element of the stabiliser on the right. Its derivative at the identity coset sends the class of XXX to TXu0T_Xu_0TX​u0​. The kernel of X↦TXu0X\mapsto T_Xu_0X↦TX​u0​ is the Lie algebra h\mathfrak hh of HHH. Indeed, a curve in HHH gives zero derivative of its action on u0u_0u0​. Conversely, if TXu0=0T_Xu_0=0TX​u0​=0, then R(exp⁡(sX))u0=exp⁡(sTX)u0=u0\mathcal R(\exp(sX))u_0=\exp(sT_X)u_0=u_0R(exp(sX))u0​=exp(sTX​)u0​=u0​ for every sss. This one-parameter subgroup lies in HHH, so X∈hX\in\mathfrak hX∈h.

The derivative on G/HG/HG/H is therefore injective. Translation by group elements gives injectivity at every coset. The inverse function theorem in local coordinates gives an immersed image with these tangent spaces. Since G/HG/HG/H is compact, its continuous injective map into the Hausdorff space RN\R^NRN is a homeomorphism onto its image. Together with the injective differential, this makes the image embedded. Rank–nullity gives its dimension dim⁡g−dim⁡h\dim\mathfrak g-\dim\mathfrak hdimg−dimh. Invariance puts every orbit point in V\mathcal VV.

Next, differentiate (17.3) in a field direction www: ∇V(R(g)u)⋅R(g)w=∇V(u)⋅w.\nabla V(\mathcal R(g)u)\cdot\mathcal R(g)w=\nabla V(u)\cdot w.∇V(R(g)u)⋅R(g)w=∇V(u)⋅w. Since this holds for every www, orthogonality gives the gradient identity ∇V(R(g)u)=R(g)∇V(u).\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:gradientcovariance} \nabla V(\mathcal R(g)u)=\mathcal R(g)\nabla V(u). \end{equation}∇V(R(g)u)=R(g)∇V(u).(17.5) At u0u_0u0​ the right side is zero. Differentiate the left side along g(s)=exp⁡(sX)g(s)=\exp(sX)g(s)=exp(sX) at zero. The chain rule gives D2V(u0)TXu0=0D^2V(u_0)T_Xu_0=0D2V(u0​)TX​u0​=0. This proves the full kernel inclusion, not only a zero quadratic form on tangent vectors.

Finally, the spectral decomposition above identifies the Hessian nullity with the number of massless linearised components. The tangent-space inclusion gives the lower bound. Equality of dimensions holds precisely when these two subspaces coincide. 

A Goldstone mode is a massless direction forced by this orbit argument. The count concerns independent continuous directions, the quotient g/h\mathfrak g/\mathfrak hg/h. Breaking a discrete symmetry does not by itself give a positive-dimensional tangent space. A Goldstone direction is tangent to a curve of vacua. The straight line with that initial tangent need not stay in the vacuum set.

17.3 The global O(2)O(2)O(2) model

Take the two real components of Lecture 2, Φ=ϕ1+iϕ2\Phi=\phi_1+\ii\phi_2Φ=ϕ1​+iϕ2​, without a factor 1/21/\sqrt21/2​. Use the scalar part of the course’s Higgs normalisation with Aem=0\Aem=0Aem​=0: L=12∂μΦ‾ ∂μΦ−λ8(∣Φ∣2−v2)2,λ>0,v>0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:17:O2} \Lag=\frac12\partial_\mu\overline\Phi\,\partial^\mu\Phi -\frac\lambda8(|\Phi|^2-v^2)^2, \qquad \lambda>0,\quad v>0. \end{equation}L=21​∂μ​Φ∂μΦ−8λ​(∣Φ∣2−v2)2,λ>0,v>0.(17.6) This is a global scalar model. Its symmetry is O(2)O(2)O(2), acting orthogonally on (ϕ1,ϕ2)(\phi_1,\phi_2)(ϕ1​,ϕ2​), and its vacuum manifold is the circle ∣Φ∣=v|\Phi|=v∣Φ∣=v. Choose u0=(v,0)u_0=(v,0)u0​=(v,0). 17.4 An accidental zero curvature

A zero Hessian eigenvalue need not be a Goldstone direction. Take the one-component potential V(s)=s4V(s)=s^4V(s)=s4 and the trivial compact group G={e}G=\{e\}G={e}. Its unique vacuum is s=0s=0s=0 and its orbit is a point, but V′(0)=0,V′′(0)=0,ker⁡D2V(0)=R.V'(0)=0,\qquad V''(0)=0,\qquad \ker D^2V(0)=\R.V′(0)=0,V′′(0)=0,kerD2V(0)=R. The linearised equation is massless even though no continuous symmetry is broken. The full restoring force is V′(s)=4s3V'(s)=4s^3V′(s)=4s3, which is nonzero for s≠0s\ne0s=0. There is no nonconstant curve of vacua through zero. The potential is not flat in a neighbourhood of its minimum.

Thus orbit directions give a lower bound, not a complete classification of Hessian zeros without the kernel-equality hypothesis. Lecture 21 must retain this distinction when it couples the phase to a gauge field. Lecture 18 first studies perturbations about a nonconstant kink.

Self-check. Lecture 15 has V(s)=12(1−s2)2V(s)=\tfrac12(1-s^2)^2V(s)=21​(1−s2)2 and the discrete symmetry s↦−ss\mapsto-ss↦−s. At the vacuum s=1s=1s=1, must there be a Goldstone mode? Compute the mass.

Answer. The orbit has two points and dimension zero, so the lemma forces no massless mode. The curvature is V′′(1)=4V''(1)=4V′′(1)=4, giving mass two.

Self-check. At the rotated O(2)O(2)O(2) vacuum uθ=v(cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)u_\theta=v(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)uθ​=v(cosθ,sinθ), identify the radial and tangential eigenvectors and their squared masses.

Answer. The radial unit vector is (cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)(cosθ,sinθ), with squared mass λv2\lambda v^2λv2. The tangent unit vector is (−sin⁡θ,cos⁡θ)(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)(−sinθ,cosθ), with squared mass zero. The Hessian is λuθuθT\lambda u_\theta u_\theta^{\mathsf T}λuθ​uθT​.

Self-check. Why is ξ=vϑ\xi=v\varthetaξ=vϑ, rather than ϑ\varthetaϑ, the canonically normalised phase variable? Does this rescaling give it a mass?

Answer. The phase kinetic term at the vacuum is 12v2∂μϑ∂μϑ\tfrac12v^2\partial_\mu\vartheta\partial^\mu\vartheta21​v2∂μ​ϑ∂μϑ. The rescaling makes its coefficient 1/21/21/2. There is still no quadratic potential for ξ\xiξ, so it remains massless.

Self-check. Which part of the orbit–Hessian argument needs a minimum, rather than only a critical point? What does V(s)=s4V(s)=s^4V(s)=s4 show about the converse?

Answer. Gradient covariance gives the kernel inclusion at any critical point. The minimum makes the Hessian positive semidefinite (nonnegative squared masses) and puts the orbit in the vacuum set; at a mere critical point the orbit consists of critical points. The quartic example has an additional zero eigenvalue without a symmetry orbit direction, so a zero Hessian does not imply symmetry breaking.

Summary

For a C2C^2C2 invariant potential, a compact Lie group with a smooth linear orthogonal action gives a vacuum orbit G/HG/HG/H. The orbit has dimension dim⁡G−dim⁡H\dim G-\dim HdimG−dimH, and every tangent lies in the Hessian kernel. With canonical kinetic terms this forces at least that many massless linearised modes. The count is exact when the kernel has no additional directions. The global O(2)O(2)O(2) model with potential (λ/8)(∣Φ∣2−v2)2(\lambda/8)(|\Phi|^2-v^2)^2(λ/8)(∣Φ∣2−v2)2 has radial mass λ v\sqrt\lambda\,vλ​v and one canonically normalised massless phase. A vanishing Hessian alone does not establish a broken symmetry or a continuum of vacua.
