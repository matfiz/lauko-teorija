Lecture 20

The non-abelian Gauss law

We derive the temporal-gauge equations with the course normalisation, prove Gauss propagation for smooth fields and compute the constraint algebra. A noncommuting homogeneous example makes the cancellations explicit.

Maxwell evolution preserves Gauss’s law because the divergence of a curl vanishes. In Yang–Mills theory, derivatives become covariant derivatives and no longer commute. We prove that their additional commutators cancel, so the non-abelian constraint also propagates.

20.1 Connection, curvature and pairing

Let III be an open time interval containing zero. We work on I×T3I\times\torus^3I×T3, with all coefficients smooth and periodic. Fix G=SU(nc)G=\SU(n_c)G=SU(nc​), nc≥2n_c\ge2nc​≥2, and its real Lie algebra g=su(nc)\mathfrak g=\mathfrak{su}(n_c)g=su(nc​) of traceless anti-Hermitian matrices.

The temporal gauge is A0=0A_0=0A0​=0.

The bracket is the matrix commutator. The positive real pairing on g\mathfrak gg is ⟨X,Y⟩=−2tr⁡(XY)\langle X,Y\rangle=-2\tr(XY)⟨X,Y⟩=−2tr(XY). 20.2 Temporal Hamiltonian evolution

With coupling g>0g>0g>0, the Yang–Mills action and its scalar density are S=1g2∫tr⁡(F∧⋆F),L=12g2∑i⟨F0i,F0i⟩−14g2∑i,j⟨Fij,Fij⟩.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:action} \begin{gathered} \action=\frac1{g^2}\int\tr(F\wedge\HodgeStar F),\\ \Lag=\frac1{2g^2}\sum_i\langle F_{0i},F_{0i}\rangle -\frac1{4g^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle F_{ij},F_{ij}\rangle. \end{gathered} \end{equation}S=g21​∫tr(F∧⋆F),L=2g21​i∑​⟨F0i​,F0i​⟩−4g21​i,j∑​⟨Fij​,Fij​⟩.​(20.2) Spatial indices run from one to three. The double sum counts each spatial curvature pair twice. The action is taken on compact time subintervals. The signature and Hodge star are those of Lecture 9.

Before setting A0=0A_0=0A0​=0, note that F0i=∂tAi−DiA0F_{0i}=\partial_tA_i-D_iA_0F0i​=∂t​Ai​−Di​A0​. Thus the momentum and scalar-potential variation are Πi=g−2F0i,δA0S=−∫⟨Πi,DiδA0⟩ vol=∫⟨DiΠi,δA0⟩ vol.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:momentum} \begin{gathered} \Pi_i=g^{-2}F_{0i},\\ \delta_{A_0}\action=-\int\langle\Pi_i,D_i\delta A_0\rangle\,\vol =\int\langle D_i\Pi_i,\delta A_0\rangle\,\vol. \end{gathered} \end{equation}Πi​=g−2F0i​,δA0​​S=−∫⟨Πi​,Di​δA0​⟩vol=∫⟨Di​Πi​,δA0​⟩vol.​(20.3) Here the variation has compact time support, and the spatial face terms from (20.1) cancel. The A0A_0A0​ equation is the Gauss constraint D:=∑iDiΠi=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:constraint} \mathcal D:=\sum_iD_i\Pi_i=0. \end{equation}D:=i∑​Di​Πi​=0.(20.4) It must be derived before removing A0A_0A0​ from the density.

In temporal gauge the spatial Hamiltonian is H=∫T3[g22∑i⟨Πi,Πi⟩+14g2∑i,j⟨Fij,Fij⟩] d3x.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:H} H=\int_{\torus^3}\left[ \frac{g^2}{2}\sum_i\langle\Pi_i,\Pi_i\rangle +\frac1{4g^2}\sum_{i,j}\langle F_{ij},F_{ij}\rangle\right]\,\dd^3x. \end{equation}H=∫T3​[2g2​i∑​⟨Πi​,Πi​⟩+4g21​i,j∑​⟨Fij​,Fij​⟩]d3x.(20.5) The canonical coordinates are the real components (Aia,Πia)(A_i^a,\Pi_i^a)(Aia​,Πia​). We use Lecture 7’s functional-derivative definition, now on smooth periodic components with their CrC^rCr seminorms for every integer r≥0r\ge0r≥0. Admissible gradients are smooth maps into that same space. The bracket is the sum of the canonical brackets over iii and aaa. The constant-matrix closure and Jacobi proof applies unchanged to these finitely many components. All functionals below have such gradients.

For the magnetic part HBH_BHB​ of HHH, use δFij=DiδAj−DjδAi\delta F_{ij}=D_i\delta A_j-D_j\delta A_iδFij​=Di​δAj​−Dj​δAi​ and antisymmetry to obtain δHB=1g2∫⟨Fij,DiδAj⟩ d3x=−1g2∫⟨DiFij,δAj⟩ d3x.\delta H_B=\frac1{g^2}\int\langle F_{ij},D_i\delta A_j\rangle\,\dd^3x =-\frac1{g^2}\int\langle D_iF_{ij},\delta A_j\rangle\,\dd^3x.δHB​=g21​∫⟨Fij​,Di​δAj​⟩d3x=−g21​∫⟨Di​Fij​,δAj​⟩d3x. Thus HAj=−g−2DiFijH_{A_j}=-g^{-2}D_iF_{ij}HAj​​=−g−2Di​Fij​. The periodic face terms cancel as in (20.1). Hamilton’s equations are therefore ∂tAi=g2Πi,∂tΠi=g−2∑jDjFji.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:evolution} \partial_tA_i=g^2\Pi_i,\qquad \partial_t\Pi_i=g^{-2}\sum_jD_jF_{ji}. \end{equation}∂t​Ai​=g2Πi​,∂t​Πi​=g−2j∑​Dj​Fji​.(20.6) These equations are meaningful without imposing (20.4). The propagation theorem explains when they also preserve the missing A0A_0A0​ equation.

20.3 Propagation and the constraint bracket

For a fixed smooth periodic parameter ε:T3→g\varepsilon:\torus^3\to\mathfrak gε:T3→g, define the smeared constraint C[ε]=∫⟨Πi,Diε⟩ d3x=−∫⟨D,ε⟩ d3x.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:C} C[\varepsilon]=\int\langle\Pi_i,D_i\varepsilon\rangle\,\dd^3x =-\int\langle\mathcal D,\varepsilon\rangle\,\dd^3x. \end{equation}C[ε]=∫⟨Πi​,Di​ε⟩d3x=−∫⟨D,ε⟩d3x.(20.7) Its parameter is independent of time. The sign agrees with Lecture 11’s abelian constraint generator.

Theorem 20.2 (Non-abelian Gauss law). Every smooth solution of (20.6) on I×T3I\times\torus^3I×T3 has ∂tD=0\partial_t\mathcal D=0∂t​D=0. In particular, zero initial Gauss constraint remains zero. The constraint generator gives δεAi=Diε,δεΠi=[Πi,ε].\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:generator} \delta_\varepsilon A_i=D_i\varepsilon, \qquad \delta_\varepsilon\Pi_i=[\Pi_i,\varepsilon]. \end{equation}δε​Ai​=Di​ε,δε​Πi​=[Πi​,ε].(20.8) For fixed smooth periodic ε\varepsilonε and ζ\zetaζ, the brackets satisfy {C[ε],C[ζ]}=−C[[ε,ζ]],{C[ε],H}=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:20:algebra} \{C[\varepsilon],C[\zeta]\}=-C[[\varepsilon,\zeta]], \qquad \{C[\varepsilon],H\}=0. \end{equation}{C[ε],C[ζ]}=−C[[ε,ζ]],{C[ε],H}=0.(20.9) Thus the Gauss constraints are first class.

Proof. Differentiate the constraint, including the time dependence of DiD_iDi​: ∂tD=∑iDi∂tΠi+∑i[∂tAi,Πi]=g−2∑i,jDiDjFji+g2∑i[Πi,Πi].\partial_t\mathcal D =\sum_iD_i\partial_t\Pi_i+\sum_i[\partial_tA_i,\Pi_i] =g^{-2}\sum_{i,j}D_iD_jF_{ji}+g^2\sum_i[\Pi_i,\Pi_i].∂t​D=i∑​Di​∂t​Πi​+i∑​[∂t​Ai​,Πi​]=g−2i,j∑​Di​Dj​Fji​+g2i∑​[Πi​,Πi​]. The last commutators vanish individually. Antisymmetry of FFF and interchange of the two summed indices give 2∑i,jDiDjFji=∑i,j[Di,Dj]Fji=∑i,j[Fij,Fji]=0.2\sum_{i,j}D_iD_jF_{ji} =\sum_{i,j}[D_i,D_j]F_{ji} =\sum_{i,j}[F_{ij},F_{ji}]=0.2i,j∑​Di​Dj​Fji​=i,j∑​[Di​,Dj​]Fji​=i,j∑​[Fij​,Fji​]=0. This proves propagation without assuming the constraint during the calculation.

Next, vary C[ε]C[\varepsilon]C[ε] at a fixed time. Invariance of the pairing gives δC[ε]=∫[⟨δΠi,Diε⟩+⟨Πi,[δAi,ε]⟩] d3x,CΠi=Diε,CAi=[ε,Πi].\begin{gathered} \delta C[\varepsilon] =\int\bigl[\langle\delta\Pi_i,D_i\varepsilon\rangle +\langle\Pi_i,[\delta A_i,\varepsilon]\rangle\bigr]\,\dd^3x,\\ C_{\Pi_i}=D_i\varepsilon,\qquad C_{A_i}=[\varepsilon,\Pi_i]. \end{gathered}δC[ε]=∫[⟨δΠi​,Di​ε⟩+⟨Πi​,[δAi​,ε]⟩]d3x,CΠi​​=Di​ε,CAi​​=[ε,Πi​].​ Hamiltonian vector fields therefore give (20.8). These are statements about the gradients and smeared observables, rather than brackets of inadmissible point-evaluation functionals.

Substitute these gradients into the canonical bracket. Pairing invariance and the derivation rule for DiD_iDi​ give {C[ε],C[ζ]}=∫[⟨[ε,Πi],Diζ⟩−⟨Diε,[ζ,Πi]⟩] d3x=−∫⟨Πi,[ε,Diζ]+[Diε,ζ]⟩ d3x=−C[[ε,ζ]].\begin{split} \{C[\varepsilon],C[\zeta]\} &=\int\bigl[\langle[\varepsilon,\Pi_i],D_i\zeta\rangle -\langle D_i\varepsilon,[\zeta,\Pi_i]\rangle\bigr]\,\dd^3x\\ &=-\int\langle\Pi_i,[\varepsilon,D_i\zeta]+[D_i\varepsilon,\zeta]\rangle\,\dd^3x =-C[[\varepsilon,\zeta]]. \end{split}{C[ε],C[ζ]}​=∫[⟨[ε,Πi​],Di​ζ⟩−⟨Di​ε,[ζ,Πi​]⟩]d3x=−∫⟨Πi​,[ε,Di​ζ]+[Di​ε,ζ]⟩d3x=−C[[ε,ζ]].​ Finally, the propagation calculation is an identity after substitution of the Hamiltonian vector field at any phase point. It needs no existence claim for a path through that point. Pairing this zero directional derivative of D\mathcal DD with the fixed parameter gives {C[ε],H}=0\{C[\varepsilon],H\}=0{C[ε],H}=0 strongly. The first bracket generally vanishes only on the constraint surface. This is the first-class condition of Lecture 11, rather than strong commutativity of non-abelian constraints. 

20.4 Noncommuting homogeneous data

Take SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2), g=1g=1g=1 and spatially constant data on the torus: A1=T1,A2=T1+T2,A3=0,Π1=T1+T2,Π2=T1,Π3=0.A_1=T_1,\quad A_2=T_1+T_2,\quad A_3=0, \qquad \Pi_1=T_1+T_2,\quad \Pi_2=T_1,\quad \Pi_3=0.A1​=T1​,A2​=T1​+T2​,A3​=0,Π1​=T1​+T2​,Π2​=T1​,Π3​=0. The curvature is nonzero: F12=T3F_{12}=T_3F12​=T3​. Both momenta displayed are nonzero. The two nonzero Gauss terms cancel: [A1,Π1]=T3[A_1,\Pi_1]=T_3[A1​,Π1​]=T3​ and [A2,Π2]=−T3[A_2,\Pi_2]=-T_3[A2​,Π2​]=−T3​. For spatially constant fields the evolution reduces to ∂tAi=Πi,∂tΠi=∑j[Aj,[Aj,Ai]].\partial_tA_i=\Pi_i, \qquad \partial_t\Pi_i=\sum_j[A_j,[A_j,A_i]].∂t​Ai​=Πi​,∂t​Πi​=j∑​[Aj​,[Aj​,Ai​]]. At the stated instant, the two nonzero derivatives are ∂tΠ1=[T1+T2,−T3]=−T1+T2,∂tΠ2=[T1,T3]=−T2,∂tΠ3=0.\begin{aligned} \partial_t\Pi_1&=[T_1+T_2,-T_3]=-T_1+T_2,\\ \partial_t\Pi_2&=[T_1,T_3]=-T_2,\qquad \partial_t\Pi_3=0. \end{aligned}∂t​Π1​∂t​Π2​​=[T1​+T2​,−T3​]=−T1​+T2​,=[T1​,T3​]=−T2​,∂t​Π3​=0.​ The kinetic contributions [∂tAi,Πi][\partial_tA_i,\Pi_i][∂t​Ai​,Πi​] vanish. The other contributions are genuinely nonzero: [A1,∂tΠ1]=T3[A_1,\partial_t\Pi_1]=T_3[A1​,∂t​Π1​]=T3​ and [A2,∂tΠ2]=−T3[A_2,\partial_t\Pi_2]=-T_3[A2​,∂t​Π2​]=−T3​. Thus the Gauss derivative cancels as claimed. The Hamiltonian density is 3/2+1/2=23/2+1/2=23/2+1/2=2, so this is not a zero-field example.

20.5 Cited Cauchy theory

The theorem above is conditional on smooth evolution. It does not construct an energy-class Yang–Mills solution. For comparison, the following local result on R3\R^3R3, rather than the torus, is cited. 20.6 Residual gauge and global gauge choices

The finite gauge transformation has the convention A′=h−1Ah+h−1dhA'=h^{-1}Ah+h^{-1}\dd hA′=h−1Ah+h−1dh, with adjoint fields transforming by Πi′=h−1Πih\Pi_i'=h^{-1}\Pi_i hΠi′​=h−1Πi​h. In temporal gauge A0′=h−1∂thA_0'=h^{-1}\partial_t hA0′​=h−1∂t​h. It preserves A0=0A_0=0A0​=0 exactly when ∂th=0\partial_t h=0∂t​h=0. The generators above therefore describe time-independent residual gauge transformations.

The Coulomb condition ∂iAi=0\partial_iA_i=0∂i​Ai​=0 also does not select a unique representative globally. On the torus, take h(x)=exp⁡(2x1T3)h(x)=\exp(2x^1T_3)h(x)=exp(2x1T3​) in SU(2)\SU(2)SU(2). It is periodic since exp⁡(4πT3)=I\exp(4\pi T_3)=Iexp(4πT3​)=I. It sends A=0A=0A=0 to A1′=2T3A_1'=2T_3A1′​=2T3​, A2′=A3′=0A_2'=A_3'=0A2′​=A3′​=0. Both potentials satisfy the Coulomb condition and have zero curvature. The periodic object here is group-valued hhh, rather than a single-valued real gauge parameter of Lecture 12. This is a Gribov copy: distinct gauge-equivalent connections obeying the same gauge condition.

A stronger global obstruction is cited: Singer, Some remarks on the Gribov ambiguity, Communications in Mathematical Physics 60 (1978), 7–12, doi:10.1007/BF01609471, treats principal bundles over the four-sphere with compact non-abelian structure group and rules out a continuous choice of one connection on each orbit in that setting. This global obstruction does not rule out local gauge slices. Lecture 21 studies the coupling to a scalar field near a vacuum.

Self-check. For the homogeneous data, what remains of ∂tD\partial_t\mathcal D∂t​D if the contribution from i=2i=2i=2 is mistakenly omitted?

Answer. The uncancelled contribution is T3T_3T3​. Both original Gauss terms and both magnetic derivative terms must be retained.

Self-check. Does first class mean that {C[ε],C[ζ]}\{C[\varepsilon],C[\zeta]\}{C[ε],C[ζ]} vanishes identically? How does the abelian limit compare with Lecture 11?

Answer. The bracket is −C[[ε,ζ]]-C[[\varepsilon,\zeta]]−C[[ε,ζ]], so it vanishes on the constraint surface but need not vanish off it. For an abelian algebra the parameter commutator is zero, giving the strongly commuting constraints of Lecture 11.

Answer. The stated result assumes sufficiently small compatible data and gives uniqueness in its specified space XXX. Residual finite transformations satisfy ∂th=0\partial_t h=0∂t​h=0.

Summary

For smooth Yang–Mills evolution on the torus in temporal gauge, Πi=g−2F0i\Pi_i=g^{-2}F_{0i}Πi​=g−2F0i​ and the constraint DiΠi=0D_i\Pi_i=0Di​Πi​=0 propagates. Antisymmetry of the curvature cancels the commutator of covariant derivatives. The smeared generator gives δAi=Diε\delta A_i=D_i\varepsilonδAi​=Di​ε and δΠi=[Πi,ε]\delta\Pi_i=[\Pi_i,\varepsilon]δΠi​=[Πi​,ε], with {C[ε],C[ζ]}=−C[[ε,ζ]]\{C[\varepsilon],C[\zeta]\}=-C[[\varepsilon,\zeta]]{C[ε],C[ζ]}=−C[[ε,ζ]]. Existence at low regularity requires the separately cited analytic theorems. Temporal gauge retains time-independent gauge freedom, and global Coulomb uniqueness cannot be assumed.
