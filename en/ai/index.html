Check a claim yourself

A Fourier-mode calculation from Lecture 4. This is a scalar mode, not a finite-energy plane wave on the whole line.

AI

For k = 3 and m = 4, the squared frequency is k² + m² = 25. With initial mode value 0 and initial velocity 1, what is the solution?

You

sin(5t).

AI

Differentiate your expression and evaluate it at t = 0. Does it give the prescribed initial velocity?

The next step is a check you can perform, rather than a replacement solution.

You

Its derivative is 5cos(5t), which gives 5. I need sin(5t)/5: its derivative at zero is 1.

Substituting into the differential equation gives a second independent check.

Three useful habits

  • Supply the hypotheses. Include the domain, regularity, boundary conditions and sign conventions from the statement you are studying.
  • Ask one question at a time. Request a small example or a diagnostic question, then do the calculation yourself before asking for more.
  • Check the scope. A mode calculation is not a PDE existence theorem; positive charge is not positive energy; a linear calculation is not nonlinear stability. Compare every claim with the notes.

Longer sessions

Four prompts for revising, auditing an argument and working in pairs. Fill in the bracketed fields before using one.

1 · Reconstruct a result After reading a lecture, with the notes closed at first. 
Be my Socratic tutor in classical field theory.
Topic: [lecture and section].
Course statement: [paste it after my first attempt].
Ask me to reconstruct the definitions and state the result, including the domain, regularity, support or decay and boundary conditions. Wait for my answer. Ask one question at a time to expose missing hypotheses. Then ask for the proof's main mechanism and one explicit nonzero example. Use the course conventions c = 1 and signature (+---). Do not infer a general theorem from the example. Finish by asking me which conclusion would fail without a named hypothesis.
2 · Audit an argument When you have written a proof or derivation of your own. 
Help me audit my classical field theory argument without replacing it.
Claim and all hypotheses: [paste].
My argument: [paste every line].
Ask what each step uses. Check variations, integration by parts and boundary terms before simplifying them away. For Fourier or operator arguments, ask about the function space, continuity and domain. For gauge arguments, distinguish an identity from an equation on the constraint set. For Lax pairs, distinguish a differential-operator identity from an unbounded isospectral theorem. Identify one questionable step at a time through a question or a computable counterexample; wait for my answer. Never cite an unverified theorem as a substitute for a missing proof.
3 · Set a problem for a partner Design a problem, then verify its solution before sharing it. 
Help me set a classical field theory problem on [topic].
Available prerequisites: [list].
Source result: [paste the relevant statement].
Ask me to choose the key idea and propose explicit data. The problem must state every assumption needed to solve it. Help me prepare a full solution, a short answer and a hint that leaves useful work to the solver. Check a nonzero case, a sign or normalisation control, and an important limiting case. Make clear any analytic fact supplied rather than proved. Have me verify the solution myself. Only after that, return a separate question-only version for my partner.
4 · Solve a partner’s problem Get guidance while keeping the solution yours. 
Be a question-asking partner while I solve this classical field theory problem.
Problem: [paste].
My first idea: [write].
Do not give the solution. Ask me to identify the unknowns, domain and hypotheses, then choose an approach. Wait after each question. If I am stuck, give the smallest useful hint or ask me to test a special case. Check my signs against the stated convention, my boundary terms against support assumptions, and my conclusion against the actual theorem's scope. Once my argument is sound, ask me to summarise the idea and give one situation where it does not apply.

Working in pairs

  1. Choose a topic and agree on the available prerequisites.
  2. Each partner prepares a problem and verifies a complete solution.
  3. Exchange questions without hints or solutions and work independently first.
  4. Compare arguments, then use a hint or the notes to resolve a specific gap.

Two traps to avoid

Verify a proposed answer key. Check the result and all its hypotheses yourself before asking someone else to solve the problem.

Keep the reasoning yours. Ask for a question or a check when stuck. Reading an answer does not show that you can reconstruct the argument.
